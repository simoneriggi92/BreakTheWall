# The harbour bell of Kessmouth

An original, fictional account written for Break the Wall. Kessmouth and its people are invented.

In the spring of 1871 a storm broke the harbour wall of Kessmouth.
Two boats sank at their moorings, and the fishermen blamed the wall, not the storm.
The council could afford one repair: the wall or the old market hall.
The harbour master, Ada Pell, argued for the wall, because without boats there would be nothing to sell.
The merchants argued for the hall, because their stalls stood in the rain.
Ada Pell won the vote by one voice, after she promised to ring the harbour bell whenever a storm was coming.

The wall was rebuilt over the summer with stone from the closed quarry at Hale.
Reopening the quarry brought twenty workers to the town, and they needed somewhere to eat.
With no market hall, the merchants moved their stalls to the quay beside the new wall.
The quay was busier than the hall had ever been, because every boat now landed its catch there.

In October the bell rang for the first storm of the season.
The boats came in early, and the merchants carried their stalls back from the quay.
Nothing was lost, and the council ordered the bell to be rung before every storm from then on.

By 1875 the old market hall had become a storehouse, and nobody asked to repair it again.
The town remembers Ada Pell for the wall; the merchants remember her for the quay.

Historians of the town disagree about the quay.
Some say the market would have moved anyway, once the railway reached the harbour in 1874.
Others point out that the stalls were already on the quay three years before the railway came.
